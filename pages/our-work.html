FOSSIL FUEL PHASEOUT

Rethinking energy systems for a cleaner, fairer, and climate-resilient future.

For CAEA, phasing out gas is not a distant policy idea — it is an urgent necessity. Pakistan's growing reliance on natural gas and imported LNG (liquefied natural gas) has trapped the country in a costly system, leading to wasted resources, financial losses, and long-term debts that burden the public for years.

This is not only an economic problem. Producing and transporting gas releases methane, a gas that heats the planet even faster than carbon dioxide, and the communities living closest to gas fields, pipelines, and power plants bear the greatest damage.

Our research shows that the power sector is the most important place to begin this shift. Imported LNG was meant to bring energy security, but instead it has made electricity more expensive and left Pakistan dependent on unpredictable global markets. As solar energy becomes cheaper and more widely available, continued investment in gas makes less and less sense — it raises costs, weakens the power grid, and adds to emissions without benefiting ordinary people.

We also hold decision-makers accountable by challenging weak environmental assessments that fail to properly account for climate risks and harm to local communities. Moving away from gas will not happen overnight, but for us, this work is about confronting the interests that benefit from the status quo and building an energy system that is fair, sustainable, and built to last.

The Life Story of Manchar Lake

A field visit exploring Manchar Lake

BY HAMNA ANWAR 28.07.2026

The kacheri was based on the case of Lake Manchar. The discussion focused on the ecological destruction of the lake, and how large development projects have affected the people, culture, and livelihoods connected to it.

Lake Manchar is located in Sindh between the Khirthar mountain range and the River Sindhu. The lake is 360 miles long and 170 miles wide. It is the biggest natural sweet water lake in Asia. The word "Manchar" comes from the Sindhi words Meeh meaning rain and Chhar meaning a vast expanse. Together, "Meeh + Chhar" means a vast spread of Rain waters.
